What Goes Into an Equity Research Report: Structure, Evidence and Valuation

A comprehensive guide to the sections of an institutional-style educational equity research report—and how students can write one that demonstrates real analytical depth.

By Harion Research · Updated 2026-10-31 · 8 min read

Why structure matters in equity research

A well-written equity research report lets a reader verify your reasoning at every step. Whether the reader is a portfolio manager, a university professor, or an interview panelist, they should never have to guess where a number came from or why you reached a particular analytical conclusion.

Professional reports follow a disciplined, familiar architecture so readers can inspect the business model, sector context, financial history, valuation assumptions, and downside risks systematically. In the Harion Equity Research Internship Program, every participant authors a complete educational case study following this exact structure—without speculative price targets or buy/sell ratings.

Section 1: Executive Summary & Company Snapshot

The opening page of your report sets the factual context. It includes the company's NSE/BSE ticker, sector classification, market capitalization segment, and a concise summary table of key historical metrics (5-year revenue and profit CAGR, latest ROE, ROCE, Operating Margin, and Debt-to-Equity ratio sourced from reported filings).

The executive summary states the central analytical questions of the report in three to four crisp paragraphs: What does the business do, what structural drivers or headwinds shape its economics, and what are the primary financial observations from its multi-year statements?

Section 2: Business Overview & Unit Economics

Before discussing stock multiples or charts, explain how the business actually works on the ground. Break down revenue by product line, service vertical, and geography (domestic vs. export). Explain who the customers are (B2B industrial buyers, government tenders, or retail consumers), how products are distributed, and what inputs (raw materials, power, skilled labor) drive the cost structure.

Include a summary of capacity, plant locations, and management background based on the company's Annual Report and corporate disclosures.

Section 3: Industry Structure & Competitive Positioning

Next, place the company inside its industry context. How fragmented or consolidated is the sector? Is demand cyclical (tied to interest rates, monsoons, or capex cycles) or non-cyclical? What regulatory frameworks, import duties, or environmental norms govern the industry?

Include a structured peer-comparison table benchmarking your subject company against three to five listed competitors on NSE/BSE using data from the Harion Research Scanner and primary filings. Compare scale, operating margins, asset turnover, working-capital days, and ROCE to show where the company holds operational strengths or weaknesses relative to peers.

Section 4: Financial Statement & Cash Flow Analysis

This is the analytical core of the report. Rather than merely listing ratios in a table without commentary, explain what changed over the last five years and why:

- Income Statement Trends: Discuss revenue growth drivers (volume vs. price realization) and gross/operating margin movements.

- Balance Sheet & Working Capital: Analyze Debt-to-Equity, Interest Coverage, Inventory Days, and Receivable Days. Highlight any buildup in capital work-in-progress or intangible assets.

- Cash Flow Conversion: Compare cumulative EBITDA and Net Profit against cumulative Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) and Free Cash Flow (FCF). Explain how capital expenditure was funded.

Section 5: Valuation Context & Sensitivity Analysis

In an educational research report, valuation is presented as a study of expectations and sensitivities, never as investment advice. Compare the company's current P/E, P/B, and EV/EBITDA multiples against its own 5-year historical range and against the peer-group median. If you include a discounted cash flow (DCF) framework, state every assumption explicitly—revenue growth, terminal margin, capex intensity, and discount rate—and show a sensitivity table illustrating how the output shifts when those assumptions change.

Section 6: Key Risks, Governance & Limitations

Every rigorous report finishes with an honest examination of what could go wrong. Document specific operational risks (raw-material volatility, customer concentration, project execution delays), financial risks (refinancing, foreign-exchange exposure, contingent liabilities), and governance considerations (related-party transactions, promoter pledges, or audit remarks). Presenting conclusions strictly as educational analysis—backed by citations to primary filings—demonstrates mature, risk-first thinking.
